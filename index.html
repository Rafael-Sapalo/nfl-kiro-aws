    Method, assumptions, limitations

    • Data: 122 games, 8,557 dropbacks, NGS tracking at 10 Hz, normalised so offense moves left → right. Route runners and coverage defenders come from PFF roles.
    • Separation = distance to the nearest PFF coverage defender. A window is separation ≥ T yards held for 0.3 s. It's a separation-based window; distance alone doesn't prove a throw was feasible.
    • New vs pre-existing: a window only counts when separation goes from < T to ≥ T after the snap. Off-coverage cushion at the snap is reported separately and never credited.
    • Expected rate comes from league routes with the same alignment (outside, slot, inline TE, backfield), coverage type (man, zone, other) and starting-cushion bucket.
    • Tracking stops 0.5 s after the throw or sack, so routes are censored when the passing phase ends. We don't infer what a receiver would have done later. The Early Window Rate isn't adjusted for a QB's time to throw, so quick-release offenses get fewer chances. The 2021 Steelers had the league's quickest median release (2.4 s vs 2.7 s), which partly explains their low rates. The Open Curves are censoring-aware, so use them for those comparisons.
    • Teammate support is an associated opening: B's nearest coverage defender (within 7 yd) moves ≥ 1 yd in the direction of teammate A (ending ≤ 12 yd from him) and gets ≥ 1 yd farther from B in the second before B's window opens. It's a sequence, not proof that A caused it, so we show it on a few inspected plays, not as a league ranking.
    • Running backs start in the backfield with large cushions (more than 99% of routes are already ≥ 2.5 yd at the snap), so they rarely create new windows. Turn on the “currently open” curves to compare them.
